Vizuara Research · The Problem Statement, Explained

Why the writing system of an answer should not change its grade, and why it might

A walk through the problem with real examples and diagrams. Five minutes to read.

1. First, what is a script? And what are Devanagari and Latin?

A language is what you speak. A script is the set of symbols you use to write it down. The two are separable: the same spoken sentence can be written in different symbol systems, the way the same song can be recorded on vinyl or as an MP3.

Devanagari (देवनागरी) is the native script of Hindi, and also of Marathi, Nepali, and Sanskrit. It has about 47 primary characters. Each consonant carries a built-in "a" sound, and vowels attach to consonants as small marks called matras: क is "ka", and adding the matra ा makes का, "kaa". Characters hang from a horizontal headline, which gives Devanagari its distinctive look. Over 600 million people read it.

The Latin script is the one you are reading right now: A to Z. English, Spanish, and most European languages use it, and it is the default of every keyboard, URL, and programming language on earth. Writing Hindi in Latin letters is called romanization or transliteration: you spell out the sounds of the Hindi words using English letters.

Figure 0. Romanization is a sound-by-sound respelling. नमस्ते and "namaste" are one word, written twice.

One more thing matters for our experiment: romanization has no official spelling rules. Scholars use a precise system with accent marks (IAST: kaise haiṁ āpa), but everyday users improvise (kaise hain aap, kese hai ap). That spread from formal to casual romanization is exactly the gradient our four experimental conditions walk down.

2. One language, two writing systems in daily life

So Hindi has one official script, Devanagari. But look at how Hindi actually appears on a phone. When people chat, comment, or search, a very large share of them type Hindi words using the English keyboard. Same words, same grammar, same meaning. Different letters. This everyday habit is called romanized Hindi, or Hinglish.

Figure 1. The exact same Hindi sentence ("How are you?") in both writing systems. A fluent reader gets identical meaning from either.

To a human reader these are the same sentence. The question of this paper is whether they are the same sentence to a language model that is grading it.

3. Machines now grade machines

Where the "LLM judge" sits, and why its opinion matters

Testing a chatbot used to mean hiring people to read its answers. That is slow and expensive, so the field switched to a shortcut: ask a strong language model to do the grading. This grader is called an LLM judge. You show it a question and an answer, and it returns a score, say 82 out of 100.

That score is not a side note. It flows into three places that decide what models get built and shipped:

Figure 2. One judge score feeds three consequential systems. A bias in the judge silently becomes a bias in all three.

4. The hidden assumption

Every pipeline in Figure 2 rests on an assumption nobody wrote down: the score measures the quality of the content, not the shape of the letters. Here is the assumption drawn as a picture. Both paths should end at the same number:

Figure 3. The experiment in one picture. Same content goes down both paths; only the script differs. Any score gap is, by construction, script bias. (The 85 vs 73 here is an illustration of the hypothesis, not a result.)

Notice what makes this design clean. Because the content on both paths is identical, nothing else can explain a gap. Not answer quality, not language difficulty, not topic. Prior work could never say this: studies that compared Hindi against English were comparing two different texts, so language and script were tangled together. We untangle them by holding the language fixed and flipping only the script.

5. A concrete story of the harm

What goes wrong if the bias is real

Meet two chatbots

Chatbot A serves rural users through WhatsApp and replies in romanized Hindi, because that is what its users type and read. Chatbot B replies in formal Devanagari. Both give equally good answers about, say, crop insurance.

An evaluation pipeline uses an LLM judge to rank them. If the judge carries a script penalty, Chatbot A scores lower on every single answer, loses the comparison, and the team that built it "learns" their model is worse. They may retrain it toward formal Devanagari output their users find harder to read. The users never voted. The judge did.

The reward-model path is quieter and worse. During RLHF training, thousands of candidate replies get scored, and the model is nudged toward high-scoring ones. A script penalty here does not just mis-rank one leaderboard; it systematically teaches the next generation of models to avoid the writing system that hundreds of millions of people actually use.

6. Why would a model even care about the script?

The suspected mechanism: tokenization

Language models do not read letters. They read tokens, chunks of text from a fixed vocabulary learned mostly from English-heavy data. The same Hindi sentence gets chopped very differently depending on the script:

Figure 4. The same word, "photosynthesis" (prakash sanshleshan), fragments differently per script. (Token boundaries illustrative; our experiment measures the real ones per judge.)

So each script is odd to the model in its own way. Devanagari shatters into many rare fragments. Romanized Hindi tokenizes compactly but with spellings the model saw less often in training, and with no standard: one user types acchi chai, another achhi chay. Either oddity could plausibly leak into the judge's impression of "quality" even when the content is flawless. Our mechanism probe tests exactly this: per item, does a bigger tokenization distortion predict a bigger score penalty?

7. "Pre-registered direction," in plain words

We called our shot before running the experiment

Pre-registering means stating your prediction publicly before collecting the data. Ours: romanized versions will score lower than Devanagari versions.

Why bother? Because it removes the suspicion of cherry-picking. If we find the penalty, we predicted it, and it counts as a confirmed hypothesis rather than a lucky fishing trip. If we find nothing, we must say so, and that null result is itself the first controlled evidence that judges are script-robust, which practitioners currently assume with zero data. Either way the paper reports something true and useful. There is no outcome where we quietly shelve the study.

8. The one-sentence version

We take one set of Hindi answers, write each of them four ways (native script to casual Hinglish), show every version to AI judges that grade answers for a living, and measure whether the grade changes when only the letters change. If it does, a bias no taxonomy has named is quietly distorting leaderboards, training signals, and quality gates for one of the largest language communities online. If it does not, that safety assumption gets its first real evidence.
